<h2 align="center">🦾 Types and Capabilities of AI</h2>
<p align="center"><em>Purpose: Introduce capability-based labels and distinguish today's task-focused systems from debated or hypothetical forms of broader intelligence.</em></p>

### Learning Outcomes
-	Describe Narrow AI.
-	Distinguish Narrow AI, AGI, and ASI as capability-based concepts.
-	Explain why AGI claims require careful evaluation.
-	Recognize that these labels do not describe one specific technical architecture.

#### 3.1 Different ways to classify AI
AI can be classified in more than one way. A capability-based classification asks how broadly a system can perform tasks. A technique-based classification asks how a system is built, for example through rule-based methods, Machine Learning, neural networks, or generative models. These classification schemes answer different questions and should not be mixed.

#### 3.2 Narrow AI (task-specific or Weak AI)
Narrow AI refers to systems designed for a particular task or a limited range of tasks. Examples include image classifiers, translation systems, chess programs, spam filters, and recommendation systems. A system may perform extremely well in its defined task without having general human-like abilities across unrelated areas. Most deployed AI applications today are best understood as narrow or task-bounded systems.

#### 3.3 Artificial General Intelligence (AGI)
AGI is a proposed concept for an AI system with broad, flexible abilities across many different intellectual tasks, rather than one narrow task. There is no universally agreed test that settles whether AGI has been achieved. Researchers and organizations may use different definitions and evaluations. Claims about AGI should be assessed by asking what capabilities are demonstrated, how reliably they work, and under what conditions.

#### 3.4 Artificial Superintelligence (ASI)
ASI is a hypothetical concept describing an AI whose intellectual abilities would greatly exceed those of humans across many important domains. It is primarily discussed in relation to possible future scenarios, governance, safety, and long-term societal consequences. It should not be presented as an existing system.

#### 3.5 Capability is not the same as consciousness
A system can produce fluent language, solve difficult tasks, or perform well on a benchmark without this alone demonstrating consciousness, feelings, intentions, or human-like understanding. These are separate philosophical and scientific questions. Describe observable capabilities and avoid unsupported claims about inner experience.

#### 3.6 Evaluating capability claims
-	Specify the task: what exactly can the system do?
-	Check the conditions: does it work only with prepared inputs or in varied real-world settings?
-	Check reliability: does it succeed consistently or only on selected examples?
-	Check transfer: can it apply skills to new tasks and unfamiliar contexts?
-	Check autonomy and oversight: what decisions can it make, and where do humans remain involved?

### Worked Example
A chess engine can defeat world-class human players at chess, but this does not mean it can independently write a school report, plan a holiday, diagnose an illness, and learn every new task. Its impressive chess ability is still limited to a defined domain. High performance in one task is not evidence of general intelligence.

### Try it: learning activity
Present case cards: a chess engine, a face-recognition system, a general-purpose chatbot, and a hypothetical system that can learn and perform a wide range of unfamiliar tasks. Learners classify what is known, what is claimed, and what remains hypothetical. Require justification based on demonstrated abilities rather than product names or headlines.

### Common Misconceptions to Avoid
-	AGI and ASI are established categories of products already available to everyone.
-	A system superhuman at one game must be generally intelligent.
-	AGI has one universally accepted definition and one simple test.
-	Capability labels tell us exactly which algorithm or architecture a system uses.

### Key Takeaways
- Capability labels describe the breadth of tasks a system can perform.
- Most deployed AI systems are task-specific or bounded in scope.
- AGI remains debated and ASI is hypothetical.
- Evaluate specific evidence and avoid treating marketing claims as scientific proof.
