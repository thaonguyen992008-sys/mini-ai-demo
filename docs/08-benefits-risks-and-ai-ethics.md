<h2 align="center">⚖️ Benefits, Risks, and AI Ethics</h2>
<p align="center"><em>Purpose: Help learners evaluate AI beyond its technical capabilities and identify principles for responsible design, deployment, and use.</em></p>

### Learning Outcomes
-	Describe potential benefits and limitations of AI.
-	Recognize privacy, bias, misinformation, safety, security, and accountability risks.
-	Explain fairness, transparency, human oversight, and responsibility.
-	Apply a simple ethical review to a real-world scenario.

#### 8.1 Potential benefits
-	Efficiency: automate or speed up some repetitive tasks.
-	Scale: analyze large volumes of data that are difficult to review manually.
-	Support: provide additional information or predictions to help people make decisions.
-	Accessibility: assist with translation, transcription, captioning, and adaptive interfaces.
-	Creativity and productivity: help people brainstorm, draft, prototype, or explore alternatives.

#### 8.2 Limitations and risks
AI systems can make errors, fail on unfamiliar inputs, reproduce biases, expose private data, or be misused. They may make decisions difficult to explain, create overconfidence, or shift work and power in ways that affect communities unequally. Risk depends on the application and the consequences of errors.

#### 8.3 Bias and fairness
Bias can enter through unrepresentative data, historical inequalities, labels, design choices, measurement, or deployment. A model trained on data that under-represents a group may perform worse for that group. Fairness is not always captured by one metric; it requires asking who benefits, who may be harmed, what differences are relevant, and how affected people can challenge outcomes.

#### 8.4 Privacy and data protection
AI systems may process personal data such as names, faces, voices, location, messages, or health information. Responsible practice includes collecting only what is needed, explaining purposes, limiting access and retention, securing data, and following applicable laws and organizational policies. Users should avoid sharing sensitive information with tools unless the use is authorized and appropriate.

#### 8.5 Misinformation and synthetic content
Generative systems can create realistic text, images, audio, and video. This can support creativity but can also be used to impersonate people, fabricate evidence, or spread misleading information. Learners should check original sources, corroborate important claims, and be cautious about content designed to trigger strong emotional reactions.

#### 8.6 Transparency and explainability
People should receive information appropriate to the context about when AI is being used, what it is intended to do, and what its limitations are. Some systems can provide explanations of influential factors, but an explanation may be incomplete or misleading. Transparency should be designed alongside privacy, safety, and security.

#### 8.7 Accountability and human oversight
Organizations and people remain responsible for how AI systems are selected, deployed, monitored, and used. Human oversight should be meaningful: the reviewer needs enough information, authority, time, and expertise to question or override a system. Merely placing a person in the workflow does not guarantee responsible oversight.

#### 8.8 A practical ethical review checklist
-	Purpose: is the system being used for a legitimate and clearly defined purpose?
-	People: who may benefit, and who may be harmed or excluded?
-	Data: is the data relevant, appropriate, secure, and representative?
-	Performance: has the system been tested in the context where it will be used?
-	Transparency: do affected people understand the system's role and limitations?
-	Human control: can a qualified person review, challenge, or correct important outcomes?
-	Accountability: who is responsible when the system causes harm or makes an error?

#### 8.9 Responsible use by students
Students should follow course and school policies, disclose AI assistance when required, verify factual content, avoid submitting generated work as their own when prohibited, and protect personal or confidential information. AI can support learning when used to explain, quiz, brainstorm, or provide feedback, but it should not replace the learner's own understanding.

### Worked Example
A school considers using an AI tool to flag students who may be at risk of failing. Ask what data the tool uses, whether it reflects unequal access to resources, how accurate predictions are for different groups, who can see the results, whether students can correct errors, and whether a teacher reviews the information before action is taken. The purpose may be supportive, but safeguards are still required.

### Try it: learning activity
Run an ethical decision workshop. Each group receives one scenario: AI-assisted student assessment, automated hiring screening, facial recognition at an event, or a chatbot giving health information. Groups use the seven-step checklist. They identify at least one affected group, one potential harm, one safeguard, and one decision that should remain accountable to a human or organization.

### Check your Understanding
  1. Name three possible benefits and three risks of AI.
  2. How can biased training data lead to unfair outcomes?
  3. Give one practical way to protect privacy when using an AI tool.
  4. Why is human oversight not meaningful if the reviewer cannot challenge the model?
  5. What is the difference between transparency and accountability?
  6. Apply two items from the ethical review checklist to a school scenario.

### Common Misconceptions to Avoid
-	AI is automatically fair because it uses mathematics.
-	Human oversight means a person only approves outputs without reviewing them.
-	A model-generated explanation is always complete and trustworthy.
-	Only developers are responsible for AI impacts.
-	AI-generated media is always easy to identify.

### Key Takeaways
-	AI can bring benefits, but outcomes depend on design, data, deployment, and governance.
-	Bias, privacy, misinformation, safety, and security require active attention.
-	Fairness and transparency must be evaluated in context.
-	Human oversight and accountability must be real, not symbolic.
-	Responsible AI is a shared responsibility across the lifecycle.
